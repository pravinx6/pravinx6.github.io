Who Actually Controls the Internet?

Technology · Research · Internet

We use the internet almost every day. We search for information, watch videos, use social media, send messages, shop online and even study through it. But have you ever wondered who actually controls all of this? Is it a company, a government, or is there someone sitting somewhere making decisions about the entire internet?

The surprising answer is that no single person, company, or government controls the internet.

The internet is a massive global network made up of millions of smaller networks that are connected to each other. This is why it is often called a "network of networks." These networks communicate with each other using common technical protocols, including TCP/IP, which help data travel between different devices and networks.

So, if nobody controls the whole internet, how does everything work together?

The answer involves several different organisations and groups, each responsible for a different part of the system.

The organisations behind the internet

One of the organisations people often hear about is ICANN, the Internet Corporation for Assigned Names and Numbers. However, ICANN does not control everything on the internet. Its role is mainly to coordinate important parts of the internet's system of unique identifiers, including domain names.

For example, when you type a website address into your browser, systems such as the Domain Name System (DNS) help connect that human-readable domain name to the appropriate internet address.

Another important organisation is IANA, the Internet Assigned Numbers Authority. IANA coordinates several important parts of the internet's addressing and protocol systems, including IP address allocations, the DNS root zone and protocol parameters.

Then there is the IETF, or Internet Engineering Task Force. It is a community of engineers, researchers and other experts who develop and maintain many of the technical standards used by the internet. The IETF publishes documents called RFCs (Request for Comments), which describe protocols, standards and other technical information.

There is also the W3C, or World Wide Web Consortium. It was founded by Tim Berners-Lee, the inventor of the World Wide Web. The W3C develops web standards that help websites and web technologies work consistently across different devices and browsers.

These organisations do not sit together and "run the internet." Instead, they are part of a much larger system involving engineers, companies, governments, researchers and other organisations.

So who really has power over the internet?

Although nobody controls the entire internet, different groups have significant power over different parts of it.

1. Governments — Law and regulation

Governments can influence the internet through laws and regulations. They can make rules for telecommunications companies, online platforms, privacy, cybersecurity and digital services. In some situations, governments can also restrict access to particular websites or services.

However, a government controlling internet access within its country is very different from controlling the entire global internet.

2. Big technology companies — Platforms and services

Companies such as Google, Apple, Microsoft and Meta have enormous influence online, but they do not own the internet.

They control important platforms, operating systems, applications, cloud services and other technologies that millions or even billions of people use.

For example, app stores can determine which applications are available through their platforms. Social-media companies can decide what content is allowed on their services and how information is presented to users.

This gives large technology companies significant influence over our online experience, even though they do not control the internet itself.

3. Telecom companies and ISPs — The gateways

Internet Service Providers, or ISPs, are the companies that connect people and organisations to the internet.

In India, companies such as Airtel, Jio and Vi provide mobile or internet connectivity to millions of users.

They operate networks, cables, mobile infrastructure and other equipment that allow us to connect to the wider internet. The quality and speed of the connection we receive can depend on the plan we choose, the technology being used, network capacity and other factors.

Without these networks, having a phone or computer would not be enough to access the internet.

4. Infrastructure providers — The backbone

The internet also depends on enormous physical infrastructure.

Undersea fibre-optic cables connect continents. Data centres contain huge numbers of servers. Internet exchange points allow different networks to exchange traffic efficiently.

Large network operators also carry huge amounts of internet traffic across countries and between continents.

This means that the internet is not something that simply exists "in the cloud." Behind the websites and apps we use are cables, buildings, servers, routers and thousands of kilometres of physical infrastructure.

Why does nobody control everything?

The internet works because these different parts are connected and depend on one another.

Technical organisations develop standards and protocols, but they do not own all the physical infrastructure.

Infrastructure companies operate networks and cables, but they rely on technical standards that allow different networks to communicate.

Technology companies create platforms, software and services that people want to use, but they depend on networks and infrastructure to deliver those services to users.

Governments can create laws and regulations, but they do not control every network, website or company around the world.

This creates a complicated system of shared responsibility and interdependence.

So, who actually controls the internet?

The simplest answer is: nobody controls all of it.

But that does not mean nobody has power.

Governments control laws and regulations. ISPs control parts of our access to the network. Technology companies control major platforms and services. Infrastructure providers operate important physical networks. Technical organisations coordinate standards and systems that allow everything to work together.

Each controls a different piece of the puzzle.

That may be the most interesting thing about the internet. What looks like one giant system is actually millions of networks, organisations, technologies and people working together.

There is no single person sitting behind a screen controlling the internet.

The internet is controlled by no one — but influenced by many.
